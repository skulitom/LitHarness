### 4.1 The items, routed — one family

All three are one thing: **the narrator's idiom is a British depot's, and the reader is not.**
*He is days* is the shift-work way of saying he works the day shift; *on a double* is a double
shift; *made up* is northern English for pleased; *three shifts a day have walked flat* has
the workers standing in for their shifts and *walked flat* for worn flat by walking, a
compression a reader who has never been on that floor cannot unpack at reading speed. Two of
the four are §181's family (E2, a specialist's word where ordinary speech has one; the clause
lived on the reviser and the reviser is off) and two are the compression §176 and read 15
named (F1, a figure that fails a literal read). The anchors do none of this: *Primal Hunter*
and *Defiance* are written in a plain international English with every term explained in
passing, and the shelf this house publishes on is read on every continent.

**Direction gap, with our own text as the cause — the §116 shape.** Draw 3's dossier told
the writer to tell it *to a mate at the bar the same night*, and a mate at the bar shares the
narrator's words; the idiom is what that listener invites. Nothing asked for a listener who
would not. The fix is the listener and not a rule about words: the same stance, told to a
mate from the other side of the world who reads these books and has never set foot in a
depot, so every word for the work is one that listener would follow — which is who the
reader is. A new dossier is a new writer (a dossier is content-addressed), declared and
accepted by hand for draw 4; `roster check` is the rail it has to pass.

**The aside underneath item 3** (*That is the horrible part*) is the confessional family §5.3
already names, unchanged by this read; it is counted, not acted on.
