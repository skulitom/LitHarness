## 2. The items, routed

### 2.1 The ladder without its prize (items 1 and 4)

*The third rung, the lowest that comes with a livery* and *the number was going to go up* are
one defect seen twice: the ladder reaches the page as a count with nothing attached to it. The
concept holds how many steps the system has and where the strongest known stands, and nothing
about **what a step buys** a person: pay, safety, power, standing. So the listing's want comes
out in the concept's furniture (*rung*, *livery*), and a character's excitement comes out as a
number going up for no reason, which is exactly our own design philosophy (§113, a countable
ladder) showing through a character's mouth. Draw1b of pilot 22 had the prize by accident
(*a Two gets paid. Proper paid. Not depot paid*) and read well for it. **Fix: the concept
carries the prize, in the words the person used before the system came, and the want is asked
for in those words.**

### 2.2 The system with a manner and no look (item 2)

The notice's appearance is given by analogy and negation (*It wasn't on a screen. It was just
there, the way a word is there when you've read it*; *No name on it*), and the operator saw
nothing. Both anchors give the box a look in one clause: a blue window with white edges; words
before his eyes. The concept asks how the system *shows itself* and the writers answered with
its voice; nothing asked what it **looks like**. The absence tic and the explaining then fill
the gap. **Fix: the concept carries the look — colour, place, light, type — as material the
seed and the writer both hold.**

### 2.3 What the writer who tells it to nobody does not explain (items 3 and 5)

*The wet bit* and *a card through the door* are read 16's family (a compression; a British
idiom for the missed-delivery slip a courier leaves), and the second is the premise's central
image, never established on the page. The far-listener dossier over-explained; the dossier
that tells it to nobody explains nothing, including the one thing that needed it. The anchors
explain in passing because a close-third narrator addresses the reader without a listener on
the page. **Routing: the person flip**, which the harness was built to measure (`--person`) and
which is the operator's position to reverse (read 4 §3, §195.2); the recommendation is in §3.

### 2.4 The supervisor with a tablet (item 6)

The seed made her *the only one on the floor who read the integration paperwork before it
arrived*, and the writer ran the minute the System landed as a kit issue. The operator's
reading — nobody would be that formal — is the family the reads have counted since draw 1 as
*nobody exclaims*: zero exclamation marks in seven draws, a world that reacts to the end of
the world with a clipboard. The arrival has no felt scale because nothing asks for one: the
concept's first arc is three events, and the threat that makes the arrival terrible is not a
field. Pilot 22's concept has no monster at all. **Fix: the concept carries the threat — what
kills people in this world in its first days, and where it first reaches the person.**

## 3. The question: what would hook him

Seven chapters under three concepts, and the register loop has done what it can: read 17's
items fell from nine to two at lower counts, and what remains is the model's first-person
default. The operator is not hooked, and the reads say why, and it is not the prose.

**Every chapter this house has drawn ends with its person worse off or merely offered
something.** A figure he cannot pay (pilot 21 draw 3); a letter and a seat on a van (draw 4);
a dead man's class at attempt three (pilot 22 draw1b); a sheet of noughts and a box (draw2); a
dead man's face in a bus-shelter panel (pilot 23). The shelf's first chapters end with the
person holding something enormous and unfair: a universe initiating; a roll at one in a
hundred thousand, won, and a red pillar; a dungeon far above his level; the Game. The reader
of this shelf is twenty to thirty and reads for the power, felt in chapter one, with the
danger beside it. Our house rules have been engineering the other way — start weak, pay for
everything, the offer unanswered — and the operator's own hook memo says what a hook is: *an
exception belonging to one person; the MC has what nobody has and progresses faster*. Our
exceptions are burdens: a dead man's weight, a headache, a jaw set wrong.

So, in order:

1. **Power in chapter one.** The exception is a power, and it works for the first time inside
   chapter one, better than anything anyone else on the page has. The concept carries the
   first use as a field the outline places.
2. **A threat, and the arrival's scale.** What kills people in this world, and where it first
   reaches the person; the seed builds it and the outline places it. A world with a threat
   reacts to its arrival the way people do.
3. **The ladder's prize**, and the want in the person's own words (§2.1).
4. **The system's look** (§2.2).
5. **The person flip, run as the A/B it was built for.** Same concept, the same writer, one
   listing and one chapter in each person, both in front of the operator. Third person is
   what both anchors are, it explains in passing without a listener, and the model's
   first-person defaults (the aside, the *you*-simile, the explaining, the chained *and*) are
   first-person defaults.

The first four are concept fields, material and not rules, shipped as §198 with the concept
stage's own tests; the fifth is a flag. None of them is a bar, and one draw is one draw.
