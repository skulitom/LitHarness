He shoved the pry bar farther underneath the trapped frame. Its tip scraped against the new stone protruding through the floor. No room for leverage, but enough room for his hand.

He put his palm against the stone and curled his fingers under the steel.

He pushed.

It was not strength exactly. His hand stayed open while the frame rose away from his palm, carrying his fingers with it. Pain tightened his forearm. The seat mounting groaned.

Jamie stared.

“Pull your leg,” Elias said.

Jamie tried. The shoe came away, but his shin stayed caught.

“Again.”

“It won’t.”

Elias looked beneath the water. The frame had twisted around Jamie’s ankle. Lifting one side tightened the other against him.

He needed to lift and turn it. He could do one with the skill, not both.

At the rear window, Saira tied the throwline around her waist.

“Keep this loose till I’m up.”

She lowered herself outside, reached across to the wall and set a bare foot on wet concrete.

For an awful second she hung between bus and wall, spread wide above the water. Then she released the bus.

Her foot held.

She climbed in short, deliberate movements. Each time she shifted a hand, her body sagged. By the third reach, her breathing was loud enough for Elias to hear.

Darren paid out rope.

A wave slapped through the broken window. The woman in the green coat made a thin, confused sound.

Jamie lifted her head higher onto the bag with his free hand.

“What’s her name?” Elias asked.

“Nessa.”

“Nessa, can you hear me?”

Her eyelids moved.

“Don’t waste it,” Jamie said. “Get someone to hold her.”

Elias called Darren over. The young man looked towards the window.

“Keep an eye on the rope,” Elias said. “But hold her head. Please.”

Above them, Saira reached the handrail. She wrapped the line around an upright and shouted for Darren to pull the other end through the bus’s rear grab rails.

They had a route now. A steep rope leading from the broken window to the ramp, with water moving beneath it.

They also had less time than Elias had promised.

The water reached the underside of Jamie’s jaw.

He tilted his head back.

Elias tried to reposition his hand. The instant his fingers lost contact, the frame dropped.

Jamie’s scream ended in a mouthful of water.

Elias drove it up again. His arm shook. He could not let go to lift his brother; he could not get his other arm underneath him without forcing Jamie’s face down.

Saira’s remaining kernel lay on a dry seat cushion.

“Darren,” Elias said. “The clear one.”

The young man pressed it into his free hand.

**SKILL KERNEL: BREATH VAULT**  
**Store up to six full breaths.**  
**Release stored air around nose and mouth while submerged.**  
**Filling requires deliberate breathing.**  
**ACCEPT?**

He had a skill already.

Everyone had one Slot. One skill. The white announcement had said so, with the authority of daylight.

Jamie coughed river water against his sleeve.

Elias accepted.

The clear oval dissolved.

**SLOT: WEDGE / BREATH VAULT**  
**CAPACITY: ∞**

He drew a breath.

Something inside his chest opened without moving. He exhaled, and the air did not leave his mouth.

He inhaled again, greedily.

“What are you doing?” Jamie asked.

“Something useful.”

“You look like you’re going to faint.”

On the third stored breath, Elias’s head began to spin. He stopped, took two ordinary breaths, then leaned his face into the water to see the trapped leg.

Air pressed cool and close around his mouth and nostrils. He could breathe. His eyes still stung; muddy water still filled his ears.

Below the seat, a bent crosspiece hooked behind Jamie’s ankle.

Elias hooked it with his free hand and pulled.

Nothing.

His bracing arm trembled. The skill did not care that he could breathe underwater. He still had only two hands.

Someone gripped his collar and hauled his head up.

Saira had come back down the rope.

“I saw you take the yellow one,” she said.

He spat water. “Yes.”

“And that was another.”

“Yes.”

Her face hardened, not with wonder but calculation.

“Can you give it to him?”

“I don’t know. I don’t think—”

“Then tell me what you can do.”

“Hold this up. Breathe underwater for a little while.”

“How little?”

“Three breaths left. Maybe less.”

“Right. Hold it up.”

She slid one arm behind Jamie’s shoulders and put her free hand into the water.

“Show me where.”

The radio on Elias’s shoulder erupted with static.

“—Venn. Elias, answer me.”

He turned his head against the transmit button. “I’m on the bus.”

Mara swore. It was brief and weary.

“Is Holt with you?”

Saira leaned towards his shoulder. “Here.”

“I need the flood relief opened locally. Control’s gone. The river’s backing into the station interceptor.”

Elias looked towards the front of the bus.

Beyond it, beneath the eastern pavement, a maintenance passage led to the manual sluice release. He had crawled through it on Thursday. At the far end, the relief channel ran beneath the underpass, joining the old outfall tunnel.

Opening the sluice would take water away from the station.

It would also send it through here.

“How many down there?” he asked.

“Last report, a hundred and forty in the concourse. They’ve got one stair clear. The lifts aren’t running.”

“Send someone to the release.”

“From where, Elias?”

He could hear water at her end too. A shouted instruction, farther away.

“I’m at the station barrier,” Mara said. “The street entrance to your access is under. You know the route from the road.”

Saira’s hand tightened on Jamie’s shoulder.

“What happens here if he opens it?”

Elias did not answer.

Mara did.

“Rapid rise, then a strong draw towards the eastern channel. Anything unsecured will move.”

“The bus?”

“Yes.”

Saira looked at him. “How long have we actually got?”

He could have said the river had changed. It had. He could have said nobody knew what those columns were doing to the levels.

Jamie watched him from inches above the water.

“I gave you the wrong number,” Elias said.

“How wrong?”

“I thought six minutes. When you asked.”

Saira did not raise her voice.

“We stood people back from that window.”

“I know.”

“We could have had three out before you got aboard.”

“I know.”

Jamie closed his eyes.

That was worse than anything Saira could have said.

Over the radio, Mara asked, “What’s trapped?”

“My brother. One other passenger injured.”

“Can they be moved?”

“The frame needs turning. I can lift it.”

“Then do it.”

Elias stared at the cloudy water around his arm. His wrist had begun to cramp. There had to be a sequence that kept everything intact.

“Get the walkers out,” he said. “Then Jamie and Nessa. I’ll go through the access, open the sluice, come back before it’s fully up.”

Saira shook her head before he finished.

“You’re not giving me another best-case number.”

“The gate takes time to rise.”

“How much?”

“Depends on the head of water.”

“How much today?”

He had no answer.

She pointed at the waterline on the windows.

“You tell me what the water does. I’ll tell you what people can manage.”

He swallowed.

“When the gate opens, the passage floods from the far end. The flow goes towards the outfall. Once it gets going, I won’t be able to walk back.”

“Can you shut it?”

“From the same wheel. Unless the mechanism jams.”

“Does it jam?”

He thought of Thursday’s inspection. Rust under the pawl housing. A timber packing piece left where no timber belonged.

“I didn’t test it under load.”

“Why not?”

“Because we’d have flooded the road.”

That, at least, was true.

Mara came back, her voice clipped by interference.

“Water’s through the ticket barriers. I can give you four minutes. After that I lose the lower stair.”

Elias looked at Jamie.

Four minutes was nothing. Four minutes was the length of a kettle boiling while his brother complained about having no clean mugs.

“We can leave me till last,” Jamie said. “Nessa goes now.”

“You’re not staying on the bus,” Elias told him.

“Neither is she.”

Saira reached down into the water again, feeling along the frame.

“Venn. If I turn this towards the aisle, what gives?”

“The mounting bolt. Maybe.”

“Not your brother’s leg?”

Elias submerged his face. A breath from the reserve eased into his mouth. He traced the steel with his fingertips, found the trapped trouser fabric, the angle of the crosspiece.

He surfaced.

“Towards the aisle. Then up.”

“Darren! Bar.”

Together, Saira and Darren set the pry bar across the adjacent seat base. Elias pushed with Wedge.

The frame rose. Saira hauled sideways.

Metal shrieked.

Jamie made no sound at all this time. He bit the heel of his hand and slid backwards as the crosspiece cleared his ankle.

Elias released the skill.

The frame fell into the water with a heavy clang.

For a moment he could only hold his useless arm against his chest.

Saira was already working.

“Darren, you go first. At the rail, help them over. You in the blue scarf, follow him. Keep the rope under your armpits. Don’t wrap it round your hand.”

The passengers began moving.

Elias crouched beside Jamie. His brother’s trouser leg was torn, the shin swelling beneath it.

“Can you stand?”

“No.”

“Try.”

Jamie looked at him.

Elias stopped. “Sorry.”

Saira came back with a second belt stripped from a passenger and the bus first-aid kit.

“We get Nessa up in a sling. Your brother waits on the back seat. Then him.”

“I should take him across.”

“You should fill up whatever lets you breathe.”

She said it without cruelty. She was already tying the belts together, testing the buckle with both hands.

Elias wanted to insist. Jamie was his brother. He had come here for Jamie.

Instead he handed Saira the radio.

“Keep this. Mara needs to know when you’re clear.”

“How will you know?”

He pointed through the broken rear window. Above the eastern access mouth, a rusted ladder rose beside the retaining wall.

“I can wait there till I see him on the rope. Then go under.”

Mara’s voice hissed from the radio. “Holt, I need that gate.”

“You’ll get it,” Saira said. “Not before my passengers are off this bus.”

Elias drew a deliberate breath and stored it.

Jamie reached for his sleeve.

“Is it safe in there?”

“No.”

His brother’s fingers tightened.

Elias waited for some reply: a joke, an accusation, permission he had no right to ask for.

Jamie said, “Then don’t do the thing where you pretend.”

Elias nodded.

He filled the rest of the reserve slowly, while Saira and a remaining passenger lifted Nessa towards the window.

Then he climbed out the front doors and stepped into water above his waist.

The current tried to turn him before he reached the access.

He worked along the bus with both hands on its panels. By the front wheel, the road dropped beneath his boot. He went under, swallowed water before he thought to release the reserve, and struck his shoulder against stone.

Air formed around his mouth.

He found the wheel arch and pulled himself upright.

Above the bus, Darren straddled the retaining-wall rail. Two passengers held his coat while he hauled Nessa up by the sling. Her green coat hung limp.

Elias reached the ladder beside the access mouth and climbed two rungs.

The maintenance passage lay below him, already submerged. Yellow paint marked its lintel beneath the surface.

He could still choose the wall. He could wait for Jamie, help Saira get him up the rope, and leave Mara with her hundred and forty people and her rising water.

The radio sounded faintly across the road.

Saira answered, but he could not hear the words.

Nessa went over the rail.

Jamie appeared in the broken window, held beneath the arms by the last passenger. Saira looped the rope across his chest and tightened a knot.

Elias raised his good arm.

Jamie saw him.

For a moment his brother looked so young that Elias could remember carrying him on his shoulders, Jamie’s trainers knocking against his ribs.

Then Saira lowered him into the water.

The rope took his weight. His face stayed clear. Darren leaned down from the wall, reaching.

Jamie was on the line.

Not safe. Not dry.

But no longer Elias’s to hold.

Elias ducked under the yellow lintel.

The passage ceiling scraped his back. He moved by touch: left hand along brick, right hand finding the steel pipe that ran towards the release chamber. Two bends. A step down. No branching until the gate.

He had told Mara the route was clear on Thursday.

It had been clear enough for an inspection.

A loose cable caught his collar. He backed up, tore himself free and felt water pulse around him.

One stored breath emptied.

At the chamber, he surfaced into a pocket beneath the arched ceiling. His helmet knocked brick. He had lost the helmet somewhere, he realised; that was his skull.

The release wheel stood half underwater.

He braced a foot against its pedestal and turned.

Nothing.

“Come on.”

His voice sounded small in the enclosed air.

He shifted his grip, used both arms. Pain burned along the forearm that had held the seat. The wheel moved a quarter-turn, then another.

Behind the grille at the chamber’s far end, the gate rose.

Water hammered underneath it.

The level around Elias’s waist climbed to his chest in seconds. The wheel dragged against his hands. He turned again.

A crack sounded inside the housing.

The gate stopped.

Water screamed through an opening no higher than his boot.

He hauled on the wheel. It would move backwards, but not forwards.

He could close it. Preserve the passage. Go back to the ladder.

Above the wheel, a pressure dial shuddered near its red mark. The station interceptor was still full.

Elias let go and submerged.

The reserve opened around his mouth. He felt along the housing until he found the obstruction: the timber packing piece had shifted into the rack. The gate’s weight had driven a splintered end between the lifting teeth and the pawl bracket.

He pulled it.

It did not move.

He pulled harder, feet against the wall. A splinter entered his thumb. The wood stayed lodged.

The gate needed to rise another few inches before it would clear the emergency catch. Beyond that point, the counterweight would take it fully open.

The wheel could not lift it with the rack jammed.

But the bottom of the rack stood within a handspan of the pedestal.

Elias fitted his hand into the gap.

Wedge pushed.

The mechanism trembled.

His fingertips slid on grease. He reset them, palm against iron, and drove upwards.

The rack moved.

Not much. Enough for the timber to crack.

The pawl bracket bent outwards.

He stopped.

That bracket held the gate against the counterweight. Break it, and there would be no winding the gate shut again. No slow opening. No passage back.

His reserve yielded another breath.

He could feel the flow seeking the outfall behind him, tugging at his clothes.

He thought of the passengers stepping away from the window because he had said ten minutes.

This time he knew what would happen.

He pushed.

The bracket broke with a shock that travelled through his wrist.

The rack shot upwards.

Elias snatched his hand clear as the wheel spun. The gate slammed into its upper stops, and the chamber became a throat.

Water hit him broadside.

He caught the pedestal with both arms. His feet streamed behind him. Above the wheel, the pressure dial’s needle dropped out of the red.

It worked.

He saw that much before the current tore him loose.

Brick scraped his shoulder. Something struck his hip. He reached for the pipe, missed it, and was carried through the outfall mouth feet first.

The dark turned him over.

He spent one breath trying not to panic and another failing.

His fingers found seams that vanished before he could close his hand. The channel widened. For a moment he rolled without touching anything.

Then his back struck a sloping surface, and the water threw him upwards.

His last stored air thinned around his mouth.

He broke the surface coughing.

A ledge caught his forearms. He dragged himself onto it, unable to get his knees beneath him, and lay with his face against cold stone.

Breath Vault was empty.

He took air that smelled of wet copper.

The chamber around him was enormous.

Black columns rose out of the water, their sides scored with the same broad steps he had seen above the warehouses. A pale light travelled beneath their surfaces. Far overhead, water fell through narrow slots with the steady roar of heavy rain.

He was beneath the new obstruction.

Behind him, the outfall emptied through a hole twice his height. Even if he could reach it, the current would not let him enter.

Across the chamber stood an open doorway.

Something moved in it.

At first Elias thought the little lights belonged to the wall. Then the shape unfolded, lifting a long forelimb from the threshold.

Kernels studded its skin. Grey ones. Yellow ones. Clear ovals set deep between overlapping plates.

Its head turned towards his coughing.

Elias pushed himself backwards until his heel found the edge of the ledge.

Above him, Jamie still needed to reach dry ground.

The only way Elias could go was past the thing in the doorway.
