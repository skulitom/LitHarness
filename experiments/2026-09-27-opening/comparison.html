Every Skill a Handhold

Chapter 1

Owen had pulled every tin to the front of the shelf except the ones behind his phone.

On the screen, a climber hung sideways beneath a plywood overhang. Owen paused the video before the move he kept getting wrong. Left foot higher. Turn the knee in. Stop trying to haul himself up by his arms.

“Owen.”

He put the phone against his thigh.

Leanne stood at the end of the aisle with an empty stock trolley. His night-shift supervisor glanced from his hand to the untouched cardboard tray beside his shoes.

“Are those going to face themselves?”

“Nearly done.”

He was twenty-four, and he still said it in the voice he’d used when his mum asked about homework.

Beyond Leanne, the main aisle ran towards the tills and the glass frontage. In the other direction, past household cleaning, a doorway opened into the rear loading corridor. Dev was back there with the floor scrubber. Its steady whine had been following Owen around the shop all night.

He wanted that sound to stop, the shift to end, and the climbing wall to open before everyone with a proper job arrived.

His phone went white.

Not bright: letters. Still white letters on a charcoal background.

EVERY PERSON HAS RECEIVED ONE SLOT.
CHOOSE WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO.

The same message stood across his sight when he looked up.

Owen blinked hard. The words remained in front of the baked beans.

“Leanne?”

“I can see it.”

At the far end of the shop, every lit till screen carried the message. Leanne lifted her own phone, then lowered it without touching anything.

Something cracked behind the cleaning aisle.

The scrubber stopped.

A second crack went through the floor beneath Owen’s shoes. It sounded like a pallet breaking under too much weight, except it kept going. Leanne caught the trolley as its wheels rattled.

From the rear doorway came a rising scrape of metal.

“Dev?” she called.

Owen stepped into the main aisle.

The floor of the loading corridor buckled. Pale tiles tilted towards each other, then burst apart around a dark, rounded mass. A root pushed through, thick as the scrubber’s body, carrying wet earth and scraps of something blue.

It struck the doorframe.

Owen backed away so quickly that he collided with Leanne’s trolley.

“Front doors,” she said. “Go.”

Another root dragged across the doorway. Behind it, a fluorescent fitting swung down on its cable and struck the wall.

There should have been a staff car park beyond that wall.

Through a widening split above the corridor, Owen saw leaves.

They were hanging against a cliff face, far higher than the shop roof. Water spilled from a stone channel through the break, warm drops striking his cheek. Above it were narrow ledges planted with small trees. A light moved behind their branches.

For a moment he could not make his feet follow Leanne.

Then the ceiling cracked, and he followed.

They reached the next aisle before a new panel opened in his sight. This one had his name.

Owen Price
SLOT COUNT: 1
SLOT CAPACITY: UNLIMITED
INSTALLED SKILLS: NONE
SLOT RANK: 1

He stopped beside a display of washing powder.

His own name made it worse. The message on the tills might have been something happening to the shop. This was happening to him.

Leanne was ahead of him, turning back.

“Keep moving.”

“What does yours say?”

“What?”

“Capacity.”

“One.” Her gaze shifted across something he couldn’t see. “One Slot. Nothing installed. Why?”

“Mine says unlimited.”

She looked at him properly then.

“Unlimited what?”

“I don’t know.”

He tried to dismiss the sheet as though closing an advert. It faded when he wanted it gone. That small obedience frightened him almost as much as the roots.

The route to the frontage was clear. Beyond the tills, the automatic doors stood open, and he could see the pavement under the streetlights. Nothing had broken there. He could get outside. He could phone someone, if phones still did that.

“Owen!”

Dev’s voice came from the rear.

Owen turned.

“Don’t leave me back here.”

Leanne had heard. She pointed towards the doors.

“I’ll check outside. Don’t get under that ceiling.”

He nodded, already going the other way.

At the loading doorway, he stopped well short of the broken tiles.

A root had lifted the corridor floor into a ridge. Beyond it, the floor had fallen away altogether, leaving a dark gap between Owen and the raised concrete landing at the staff exit. Dev crouched on that landing beside the scrubber. The machine had tipped against the exit door.

“Can you shift it?” Owen called.

“Tried. Door won’t move anyway.”

Dev held one arm across his middle. There was blood on his sleeve.

Along the left side of the corridor, the painted block wall still joined doorway to landing. Owen traced it automatically: start low, move sideways, keep his weight over his feet.

There were no holds.

No projecting bricks, no pipe he could trust. Just smooth paint above the torn edge of the floor. At the climbing wall, he would have laughed at someone calling it a route.

A quiet choice appeared when he thought of the Slot.

CLING, Controlled adhesion through hands and feet.
ANCHOR, Hold your own position against external force.
KINDLE, Ignite suitable material by touch.

Owen read the first line again.

He put his palm against the corridor wall. Ordinary paint. Ordinary skin. When he leaned, his hand slid.

Across the gap, Dev watched him.

“Can you get over?”

Owen looked at the word Cling, and his mouth went dry.

“I might be able to.”

Anchor sounded safer. Hold your position. Stay where you were while everything else went wrong.

But Dev was on the other side of the break. Staying here would not help him, and Owen could see nothing useful to set on fire.

He chose Cling before he could talk himself out of it.

[STATUS] Owen Price — Cling 1

The other choices vanished.

His palm remained against the painted blocks. Nothing tingled. He could still feel grit beneath his skin and the cool wall beneath that.

Then he tried to slide his hand.

It stayed.

Owen pulled back sharply. His palm released as soon as he wanted it to, leaving him staring at his fingers.

“What happened?” Dev called.

“I took Cling.”

“Does it work?”

He pressed his hand flat again and leaned away. The skin across his palm tightened. His wrist took the pull.

“Yes.” He swallowed. “I think so.”

Footsteps stopped behind him. Leanne had returned to the loading doorway, keeping clear of the broken tiles.

“Pavement’s clear outside,” she said. “What are you doing?”

“Going along the wall.”

Her eyes followed the blank paint to Dev’s landing.

“With what?”

“This.”

He lifted a foot and pressed the sole against the wall. It held when he put weight through it. Not on a ledge, not against a lump in the paint. Just there.

For an instant he wanted Leanne to see exactly how impossible that was.

Then a tile dropped from the broken floor and he heard it hit something far below.

“Can you do the same?” Owen asked Dev.

“Already picked Anchor. When the floor started moving.”

Dev shifted his free hand from the landing, then planted it again.

“It’s keeping me here.”

“Right. Stay there.”

As instructions went, it was useless. Dev nodded anyway.

Owen put both hands against the wall and brought his other foot up. Now nothing beneath him belonged to the floor.

His stomach lurched. He kept his eyes on the paint.

At the climbing wall, starting a traverse was something he did while talking. Here he spent too long checking each contact, until his shoulders began to ache before he had moved beyond the doorway.

Feet first. Stand on them.

He edged sideways, releasing one shoe and placing it farther along. The adhesion returned when he wanted it. His body seemed to understand that much. It did not understand where to put the rest of him.

He reached too far with his right hand.

His hips swung away from the wall. Weight dragged through his outstretched arm, and pain caught at the base of his wrist.

“Owen,” Leanne said.

“I know.”

He didn’t, for a moment. His left foot was too far behind him, his right knee bent beneath his chest. He tried to pull himself closer and made the pain worse.

Stop hauling.

He brought the trailing foot underneath his hip. Turned the knee out. Pushed through the leg until his body rose and the load eased off his hand.

There.

He stayed still long enough to breathe.

“You all right?” Leanne asked.

“Keep talking to Dev.”

She did. Asked where he was hurt, whether he could feel his foot. Owen listened without trying to answer. A leg injury. Something sharp when the floor broke. Yes, Dev could move his toes.

The landing came closer in short, careful movements. Owen kept his arms almost straight and his steps small. He wanted to look down each time loose grit fell beneath his shoes. He looked instead at the next place his hand would go.

At last he could put a foot on concrete.

He tested it, then stepped onto the landing beside Dev and released the wall.

The words caught his eye as his weight left his hands.

Two. He had been one before.

There was no rush of strength. His wrist still hurt. But when he steadied himself against the wall, the grip felt less precarious, as though he no longer had to hold the intention quite so tightly.

“You actually did it,” Dev said.

Owen crouched beside him.

Blood had soaked the trouser leg below Dev’s knee. His sleeve was wet where he had pressed it against the wound. Owen could see a cut through the torn fabric, but not how deep it went.

“Can you keep pressure on that?”

“Been trying.”

“I know. Sorry.”

A metal maintenance rack stood between the scrubber and the side wall. Owen had to turn sideways to reach it. Beneath bottles and a battered toolbox lay a coil of braided rope, dirty at the loose end but apparently whole.

He lifted it and worked the nearest length through his hands. No obvious cuts. That was all he knew how to check.

“I’ve got rope,” he called.

“Bring an end back,” Leanne said. “I’ve taken Anchor. There’s an upright by the doorway we can use as well.”

Owen looked across. She stood inside the shop with one hand around the steel upright, away from the fractured edge.

He pictured Dev trying to follow his route with an injured leg and no grip on the wall.

“Don’t just hold him yourself,” he said.

“I wasn’t planning to.”

He nodded, embarrassed and grateful at once.

With the coil over his shoulder, he moved to the edge of the landing. His hands were shaking now. He flexed them, watching the wall he had crossed.

A crack ran through the paint.

Owen stepped back.

The blocks in the middle of his route bulged inward. Mortar spilled into the gap. Then a whole section broke loose and fell, taking his imagined footholds with it.

Dust struck his face.

When it cleared, the wall ended short of his reach. Beyond the missing section, Leanne still stood by the upright.

Owen gripped the rope.

He had brought it as far as Dev. It was still on the wrong side.

“Back from the edge,” Leanne called.

Owen moved until his shoulder touched the tipped scrubber. Dev sat between it and the wall, pressing his sleeve against his leg. The rope hung heavy on Owen’s shoulder.

The missing wall opened onto wet stone and tangled roots. The surviving section on Leanne’s side looked horribly close. Close enough to imagine catching; too far to reach.

Something pale knocked against the landing.

A slender root lay across the broken concrete beside the scrubber. Wedged around a fork in it was a ceramic sleeve, blue beneath the dust. Inside sat a translucent seed with a branching pattern suspended in its centre.

Owen crouched. The sleeve had a smooth rim and little holes spaced around its base. Someone had made it.

“Careful,” Dev said.

“I’m just looking.”

He touched the seed.

AIR STEP
A brief foothold on empty air.
Recover your breath before reuse.
INSTALL?

His hand stopped.

Beyond the broken wall, there was nowhere to put the foot that would push him across. Now the words offered exactly that.

“What is it?” Dev asked.

“A skill. Air Step.”

“Instead of sticking?”

Owen looked at the wall he needed to catch.

If Cling went away halfway across, a foothold would only let him jump into the remaining blocks before falling. He brought his sheet into view. One Slot. Unlimited capacity. Cling still listed beneath installed skills.

Unlimited had looked like a mistake beside the washing powder.

He wanted it to mean something now.

“Mine might keep both,” he said.

“Might?”

There wasn’t a better answer.

Owen chose to install.

The seed collapsed beneath his fingertip into a faint dust that disappeared before it reached the ceramic rim. The empty sleeve stayed on the root.

[STATUS] Owen Price — Air Step 1

Cling remained on his sheet beside Air Step. His rank had not changed.

He pressed his palm to the wall and pulled gently away. His skin tightened; his hand held.

“Still got it,” he said.

Dev let out a breath.

Owen wished that were enough.

He put the coil down and fed a length towards Dev, leaving plenty loose on the landing. He would carry the end rather than risk the whole coil catching on the broken wall.

“Don’t tie it to yourself,” he said. “Let it run after me.”

“You’re going to jump?”

“Not from here. I can get closer along what’s left.”

Leanne watched from beside the upright across the gap. He told her what the seed had offered. Saying it aloud made the plan sound worse.

“Can you get back if it doesn’t work?” she asked.

“Here, yes.”

He turned to Dev. “Are you still holding yourself?”

Dev nodded, his good hand flat on the concrete. “Go on.”

Owen kept one shoe on the landing and fixed both palms to the wall. He extended his other foot over the gap.

The place for it came as naturally as the intention to release Cling. There was resistance beneath his sole, flat and firm.

He hesitated.

When he finally pushed down, the support was gone.

His foot dropped. His hands caught his weight, and pain flared through the wrist he had already strained. He dragged his knee back onto the landing.

“Owen?”

“Stayed here,” he managed.

He bent over his knee. His chest felt as though he had just finished a hard problem at the climbing wall. When he tried to make another foothold, nothing met his shoe.

Recover your breath.

He waited, furious with himself for spending strength on a step he hadn’t taken.

It had felt solid. That was the useful part. He had waited for proof instead of moving, and used up the moment in which it could help him.

He shook out one hand, then the other.

“Don’t wait on it,” he said.

“What?” Dev asked.

“Me. Talking to me.”

His breathing eased. Keeping his hands low and his weight over the landing, he tried again. This time he placed the foot and pressed immediately.

His body rose. He brought his knee onto the concrete before the foothold vanished.

Not a staircase. One push.

Across the gap, Leanne lowered herself beside the upright, ready for the rope.

Owen tucked the rope end through his belt loosely enough to pull free. Then he climbed onto the remaining wall.

Feet close beneath his hips. Arms long. He knew these things. Fear kept trying to make him forget them.

At the broken edge, he looked once at the landing behind him. Dev had the loose rope clear of the scrubber.

Owen recovered his breath.

He needed to leave the wall before he could push towards the far section. For a moment there would be nothing holding him.

He released.

His stomach lifted. He placed his foot into emptiness and drove through the brief resistance beneath it.

The far wall struck his palms harder than he expected. Cling caught. His body swung below his hands, rope dragging at his belt.

Feet.

He slapped one sole against the blocks, brought the other underneath him and pushed his hips towards the wall.

His grip steadied. The pain in his wrist did not disappear.

Both. He had used both.

“Owen, come down.”

Leanne’s voice drew his eyes to the solid floor beneath her. He traversed the last stretch and stepped down beside the upright.

She caught his elbow. Only when she let go did he realise he was shaking.

They passed the rope around the steel. Owen made close turns and secured the end while Leanne checked that each turn sat against the upright rather than broken masonry. She took the loaded length with the steel between her and the gap, planted her feet and used Anchor.

“Slowly,” she called to Dev. “Pull against it first.”

The rope straightened. Nothing shifted at their end.

Dev could now pull towards them without asking an injured leg to find an invisible foothold. They still had to get him off the landing.

Owen looked at the line, then at his aching hands.

“Right,” Leanne said. “How do we support his leg?”

“Something under his leg,” Owen said. “Not just a rope round his middle.”

Across the gap, Dev looked down at the line in his hands.

“I’d quite like something under the rest of me.”

The joke made Owen breathe out. He had been staring at the drop again.

There were folded decorators’ sheets on the maintenance rack. Dev could reach them without leaving the landing. With Leanne calling instructions, he dragged one over, folded it into a broad strip and worked it beneath his thighs. His face tightened when he moved the injured leg.

“I need to go back.”

Leanne looked at his hands.

“To help him tie it,” he said. “Then I come back before you pull.”

“Can you?”

He wanted to say yes quickly.

“After a minute.”

She let him have it.

The return crossing was no less frightening for knowing it could work. He waited until his breathing settled, climbed to the broken edge and committed his weight to the air foothold without testing it first. His palms caught the surviving wall. He brought his feet up before his hips could swing far.

On the landing, he rested his forehead against the blocks.

“You don’t have to look pleased about it,” Dev said.

“Wasn’t planning to.”

They made a padded seat from the folded sheet, held by broad rope loops beneath Dev’s thighs and behind his back. Owen knew knots from the climbing wall, but this was not a harness. He checked every knot, then had Dev lean into it while the concrete still supported him.

The rope tightened towards Leanne’s upright.

“Anything slipping?” she called.

“No,” Dev said. “Digging in, though.”

They adjusted the cloth. Owen folded another sheet beneath Dev’s injured calf and secured it to the seat so the lower leg would not hang loose. Dev could still press his bloodied sleeve against the wound.

“We’ll keep you as upright as we can,” he said.

“Just get me over.”

“Let your Anchor go when you’re ready,” Leanne called.

Dev shuffled to the edge.

For a moment nothing happened.

“Got you,” Leanne said, strained now. “Keep your hands where they are.”

“Pull,” he said.

Leanne kept hold of the rope until Dev was completely on the floor.

Then she released it and sat down hard.

“Oh,” she said.

Owen looked up.

“My rank’s gone to two.”

Her hands were shaking too.

That helped, somehow. Not that she was frightened. That she had done it while frightened, and he had been allowed to see.

Owen barely noticed the shelves he had been meant to face. At the frontage, Leanne settled Dev on the pavement against the brick pier between the windows, then fetched the first-aid box from behind the tills.

Outside, the streetlights still worked.

Owen found that harder to understand than darkness would have been. The pavement continued past the shop. A parked car sat where a parked car ought to sit. Above the rear roofline rose a cliff that had no business being there.

“Hand,” Leanne said.

She had dressed Dev’s cut. Now she crouched in front of Owen.

“It’s mostly—”

He nearly tucked it beneath his other arm. She had seen him cross empty space. He did not want to spoil that by needing help with a wrist.

Then he remembered her shaking hands around the rope.

“This one.”

She asked him to move his fingers and tell her where it hurt. He did. When she told him to rest it rather than keep testing his grip, he stopped.

“You got him out,” she said quietly.

“You pulled.”

“Yes. I did.” She glanced towards the broken roof. “Next time you go somewhere, we agree how you’re coming back.”

He followed her gaze.

Above the torn metal were terraces edged with low handrails. Small trees stood in deliberate rows, their branches pruned clear of narrow paths. A light moved behind them, vanished, then appeared farther along. Another remained beside a dark opening in the cliff.

People. Or something that built paths and carried lights.

His stomach remembered the crossing. Beneath that fear came a pleasure he could not quite suppress: his foot meeting nothing and finding support; his hands catching; a route existing because he could use both.

“There might be help up there,” he said. “Food. Someone who knows what those seeds are.”

Leanne looked at him until he added, “Not yet.”

His arms still trembled. Dev needed more than a dressing. The shop behind them still held supplies they could reach without climbing anything.

But Owen kept watching the moving light.

When he went up, he would have to find a way down that someone else could use.

One Slot, Open Water

The river rose while Elias had his hand down a drain.

One moment he was feeling for the broken edge of a silt basket. The next, water punched up his sleeve hard enough to knock his elbow against the kerb. He pulled free, scattering the screws from the inspection cover.

Across the road, the number 38 stopped halfway through the underpass. Its brake lights stained the water red.

Then something white passed through the world.

It left no afterimage. It left words.

**ALLOCATION COMPLETE: HUMANITY**
**ONE PERSON. ONE SLOT.**
**STANDARD CAPACITY: ONE SKILL.**

Elias blinked. The words stayed exactly where they were, whether he looked at the road or his own hands.

Below them, smaller:

**ELIAS VENN**
**SLOT: EMPTY**
**CAPACITY: ∞**

His phone began ringing inside his waterproofs.

Beyond the underpass, above the roofs of the riverside warehouses, stood a wall that had not been there before. Black columns crossed the river from bank to bank, rising higher than the cranes. Water burst white between them. Something like a staircase climbed their outer faces, each step large enough to hold a house.

The phone stopped, then started again.

Jamie.

Elias answered with his wet thumb. “Where are you?”

“On the bus.”

“I can see it.”

“There’s something through the floor.”

Elias was already running for the council van.

“Can you get to a door?”

A pause. In it, Elias heard an engine labouring and a woman saying, sharply, “Leave the bag.”

“Not really,” Jamie said.

Elias took the throwline from behind the driver’s seat, then the long pry bar. His maintenance radio crackled on its charging cradle.

“Venn. Stay clear of the lower carriageway. We’re losing river levels. Acknowledge.”

Mara Bell. He snatched the radio too.

On the phone, Jamie drew a breath that broke halfway through.

“My leg’s under something.”

“Which leg?”

“What difference does that make?”

A good sign. Jamie still had enough air to be seventeen.

“Keep talking. I’m coming.”

He left the inspection cover open.

By the time he reached the bus, the water was above his knees. It flowed uphill towards him, pressing floating leaves against his thighs. On the far side, pale new stone had erupted through the carriageway and the bus’s underside. The vehicle leaned towards it.

The front doors opened six inches, struck the stone, and stopped.

Through the gap, the driver looked down at him. She was a broad woman with a white scar cutting through one eyebrow. Her name badge read SAIRA HOLT.

“Are you drainage?”

He looked down at his council jacket as if he might need to check.

“Yes.”

“How long before this fills?”

He knew the underpass catchment. He had spent three weeks inspecting it after the disciplinary meeting, jobs his supervisor could check without leaving the office. The river outlet had no effective non-return flap. He had written that down this time.

Water was entering through every drain he could see.

Six minutes to the window sills, perhaps less. Nobody’s calculations included a wall across the river.

“Ten minutes,” he said. “Maybe twelve.”

Her eyes moved from his face to the current.

“We were going out the rear window. It’s the high end.”

“My brother’s trapped in the middle.”

“I have another passenger down.”

“I’ve got a line. We can get everyone out.”

“How?”

“Rear window to the north ramp. There’s a handrail above the retaining wall.”

She considered the steep concrete, the depth of water between it and the bus. Then she turned.

“Darren, stop at the window. Don’t jump. We’re rigging a rope. Everyone who can stand, move forward two rows and give us room.”

The young man holding the emergency hammer lowered it. Three passengers who had been preparing to climb out stepped back into the bus.

Elias had bought Jamie time with theirs.

Saira squeezed through the doors sideways and dropped into the water. “I’ll get you in at the back. Don’t tell me ten minutes again unless you know.”

He followed her along the bus, the pry bar dragging at his wrist.

At the broken rear window, two passengers reached down. He passed up the equipment, found a foothold on the bumper and let them haul him through.

Inside smelled of hot wiring and river mud.

Jamie sat crookedly between two rows, his council-college lanyard stretched tight beneath a seat bracket. The floor had buckled upwards. A seat frame pinned his left shin against the edge of the raised section.

He looked at Elias’s jacket.

“Did you inspect this bit?”

“No.”

That answer came out too quickly.

Beside Jamie, a woman in a green coat lay with her head on a shopping bag. Her right trouser leg was dark with blood. Jamie had pressed his folded hoodie against her thigh.

“Help her first,” he said.

Elias put the pry bar under the frame.

“First I get this off you.”

“You always do that.”

“What?”

“Say what you’re doing like that makes it the right thing.”

Elias leaned on the bar. The frame lifted a fraction. Jamie screamed.

He stopped.

Under his boots, the bus shifted another inch.

Saira came back through the rear window carrying three small objects in her closed fist.

“They were in that stuff outside,” she said.

She opened her hand. A grey bead, a yellow piece shaped like a tooth, and a clear oval lay against her skin. All three looked cut from glass, though none caught the light quite properly.

The grey bead rolled against her thumb.

She went still.

“What?” Elias asked.

“It says I can stick to things.”

The other passengers watched her. Beyond the shattered window, car horns sounded without stopping.

“Who says?” Darren asked.

“The same bastard who’s writing in my eyes.”

Elias touched the yellow tooth.

The bus vanished behind a panel of words.

**SKILL KERNEL: WEDGE**
**Drive apart two solid surfaces touched by one hand.**
**Maximum travel: one handspan.**
**Requires maintained contact and exertion.**
**ACCEPT?**

He jerked his hand back. The words disappeared.

Saira was examining the grey bead.

“Cling,” she said. “Hands and feet. Doesn’t work through shoes.”

“Could you climb the wall?”

“If it’s telling the truth.”

She looked towards the rear window, where the north retaining wall rose beyond eight feet of dark water.

The grey bead crumbled into light between them.

“All right,” she said, breathing harder. “That’s something.”

She took off her shoes and tucked them under a seat. Her socks followed.

Darren laughed once, an astonished little sound.

“Save it,” she told him. “You’re on rope.”

**ACCEPT?**

Yes.

**CAPACITY: ∞**

He pushed.

“It won’t.”

“Keep this loose till I’m up.”

She lowered herself outside, reached across to the wall and set a bare foot on wet concrete.

Her foot held.

She climbed in short, deliberate movements. Each time she shifted a hand, her body sagged. By the third reach, her breathing was loud enough for Elias to hear.

Darren paid out rope.

A wave slapped through the broken window. The woman in the green coat made a thin, confused sound.

Jamie lifted her head higher onto the bag with his free hand.

“What’s her name?” Elias asked.

“Nessa.”

“Nessa, can you hear me?”

Her eyelids moved.

“Don’t waste it,” Jamie said. “Get someone to hold her.”

Elias called Darren over. The young man looked towards the window.

“Keep an eye on the rope,” Elias said. “But hold her head. Please.”

Above them, Saira reached the handrail. She wrapped the line around an upright and shouted for Darren to pull the other end through the bus’s rear grab rails.

They had a route now. A steep rope leading from the broken window to the ramp, with water moving beneath it.

They also had less time than Elias had promised.

The water reached the underside of Jamie’s jaw.

He tilted his head back.

Elias tried to reposition his hand. The instant his fingers lost contact, the frame dropped.

Jamie’s scream ended in a mouthful of water.

Elias drove it up again. His arm shook. He could not let go to lift his brother; he could not get his other arm underneath him without forcing Jamie’s face down.

Saira’s remaining kernel lay on a dry seat cushion.

“Darren,” Elias said. “The clear one.”

The young man pressed it into his free hand.

**SKILL KERNEL: BREATH VAULT**
**Store up to six full breaths.**
**Release stored air around nose and mouth while submerged.**
**Filling requires deliberate breathing.**
**ACCEPT?**

He had a skill already.

Everyone had one Slot. One skill. The white announcement had said so, with the authority of daylight.

Jamie coughed river water against his sleeve.

Elias accepted.

The clear oval dissolved.

**SLOT: WEDGE / BREATH VAULT**
**CAPACITY: ∞**

He drew a breath.

Something inside his chest opened without moving. He exhaled, and the air did not leave his mouth.

He inhaled again, greedily.

“What are you doing?” Jamie asked.

“Something useful.”

“You look like you’re going to faint.”

On the third stored breath, Elias’s head began to spin. He stopped, took two ordinary breaths, then leaned his face into the water to see the trapped leg.

Air pressed cool and close around his mouth and nostrils. He could breathe. His eyes still stung; muddy water still filled his ears.

Below the seat, a bent crosspiece hooked behind Jamie’s ankle.

Elias hooked it with his free hand and pulled.

Nothing.

His bracing arm trembled. The skill did not care that he could breathe underwater. He still had only two hands.

Someone gripped his collar and hauled his head up.

Saira had come back down the rope.

“I saw you take the yellow one,” she said.

He spat water. “Yes.”

“And that was another.”

“Yes.”

Her face hardened, not with wonder but calculation.

“Can you give it to him?”

“I don’t know. I don’t think—”

“Then tell me what you can do.”

“Hold this up. Breathe underwater for a little while.”

“How little?”

“Three breaths left. Maybe less.”

“Right. Hold it up.”

She slid one arm behind Jamie’s shoulders and put her free hand into the water.

“Show me where.”

The radio on Elias’s shoulder erupted with static.

“—Venn. Elias, answer me.”

He turned his head against the transmit button. “I’m on the bus.”

Mara swore. It was brief and weary.

“Is Holt with you?”

Saira leaned towards his shoulder. “Here.”

“I need the flood relief opened locally. Control’s gone. The river’s backing into the station interceptor.”

Elias looked towards the front of the bus.

Beyond it, beneath the eastern pavement, a maintenance passage led to the manual sluice release. He had crawled through it on Thursday. At the far end, the relief channel ran beneath the underpass, joining the old outfall tunnel.

Opening the sluice would take water away from the station.

It would also send it through here.

“How many down there?” he asked.

“Last report, a hundred and forty in the concourse. They’ve got one stair clear. The lifts aren’t running.”

“Send someone to the release.”

“From where, Elias?”

He could hear water at her end too. A shouted instruction, farther away.

“I’m at the station barrier,” Mara said. “The street entrance to your access is under. You know the route from the road.”

Saira’s hand tightened on Jamie’s shoulder.

“What happens here if he opens it?”

Elias did not answer.

Mara did.

“Rapid rise, then a strong draw towards the eastern channel. Anything unsecured will move.”

“The bus?”

“Yes.”

Saira looked at him. “How long have we actually got?”

He could have said the river had changed. It had. He could have said nobody knew what those columns were doing to the levels.

Jamie watched him from inches above the water.

“I gave you the wrong number,” Elias said.

“How wrong?”

“I thought six minutes. When you asked.”

Saira did not raise her voice.

“We stood people back from that window.”

“I know.”

“We could have had three out before you got aboard.”

“I know.”

Jamie closed his eyes.

That was worse than anything Saira could have said.

Over the radio, Mara asked, “What’s trapped?”

“My brother. One other passenger injured.”

“Can they be moved?”

“The frame needs turning. I can lift it.”

“Then do it.”

Elias stared at the cloudy water around his arm. His wrist had begun to cramp. There had to be a sequence that kept everything intact.

“Get the walkers out,” he said. “Then Jamie and Nessa. I’ll go through the access, open the sluice, come back before it’s fully up.”

Saira shook her head before he finished.

“You’re not giving me another best-case number.”

“The gate takes time to rise.”

“How much?”

“Depends on the head of water.”

“How much today?”

He had no answer.

She pointed at the waterline on the windows.

“You tell me what the water does. I’ll tell you what people can manage.”

He swallowed.

“When the gate opens, the passage floods from the far end. The flow goes towards the outfall. Once it gets going, I won’t be able to walk back.”

“Can you shut it?”

“From the same wheel. Unless the mechanism jams.”

“Does it jam?”

He thought of Thursday’s inspection. Rust under the pawl housing. A timber packing piece left where no timber belonged.

“I didn’t test it under load.”

“Why not?”

“Because we’d have flooded the road.”

That, at least, was true.

Mara came back, her voice clipped by interference.

“Water’s through the ticket barriers. I can give you four minutes. After that I lose the lower stair.”

Elias looked at Jamie.

Four minutes was nothing. Four minutes was the length of a kettle boiling while his brother complained about having no clean mugs.

“We can leave me till last,” Jamie said. “Nessa goes now.”

“You’re not staying on the bus,” Elias told him.

“Neither is she.”

Saira reached down into the water again, feeling along the frame.

“Venn. If I turn this towards the aisle, what gives?”

“The mounting bolt. Maybe.”

“Not your brother’s leg?”

Elias submerged his face. A breath from the reserve eased into his mouth. He traced the steel with his fingertips, found the trapped trouser fabric, the angle of the crosspiece.

He surfaced.

“Towards the aisle. Then up.”

“Darren! Bar.”

Together, Saira and Darren set the pry bar across the adjacent seat base. Elias pushed with Wedge.

The frame rose. Saira hauled sideways.

Metal shrieked.

Jamie made no sound at all this time. He bit the heel of his hand and slid backwards as the crosspiece cleared his ankle.

Elias released the skill.

The frame fell into the water with a heavy clang.

For a moment he could only hold his useless arm against his chest.

Saira was already working.

“Darren, you go first. At the rail, help them over. You in the blue scarf, follow him. Keep the rope under your armpits. Don’t wrap it round your hand.”

The passengers began moving.

Elias crouched beside Jamie. His brother’s trouser leg was torn, the shin swelling beneath it.

“Can you stand?”

“No.”

“Try.”

Jamie looked at him.

Elias stopped. “Sorry.”

Saira came back with a second belt stripped from a passenger and the bus first-aid kit.

“We get Nessa up in a sling. Your brother waits on the back seat. Then him.”

“I should take him across.”

“You should fill up whatever lets you breathe.”

She said it without cruelty. She was already tying the belts together, testing the buckle with both hands.

Elias wanted to insist. Jamie was his brother. He had come here for Jamie.

Instead he handed Saira the radio.

“Keep this. Mara needs to know when you’re clear.”

“How will you know?”

He pointed through the broken rear window. Above the eastern access mouth, a rusted ladder rose beside the retaining wall.

“I can wait there till I see him on the rope. Then go under.”

Mara’s voice hissed from the radio. “Holt, I need that gate.”

“You’ll get it,” Saira said. “Not before my passengers are off this bus.”

Elias drew a deliberate breath and stored it.

Jamie reached for his sleeve.

“Is it safe in there?”

“No.”

His brother’s fingers tightened.

Elias waited for some reply: a joke, an accusation, permission he had no right to ask for.

Jamie said, “Then don’t do the thing where you pretend.”

Elias nodded.

He filled the rest of the reserve slowly, while Saira and a remaining passenger lifted Nessa towards the window.

Then he climbed out the front doors and stepped into water above his waist.

The current tried to turn him before he reached the access.

He worked along the bus with both hands on its panels. By the front wheel, the road dropped beneath his boot. He went under, swallowed water before he thought to release the reserve, and struck his shoulder against stone.

Air formed around his mouth.

He found the wheel arch and pulled himself upright.

Above the bus, Darren straddled the retaining-wall rail. Two passengers held his coat while he hauled Nessa up by the sling. Her green coat hung limp.

Elias reached the ladder beside the access mouth and climbed two rungs.

The maintenance passage lay below him, already submerged. Yellow paint marked its lintel beneath the surface.

He could still choose the wall. He could wait for Jamie, help Saira get him up the rope, and leave Mara with her hundred and forty people and her rising water.

The radio sounded faintly across the road.

Saira answered, but he could not hear the words.

Nessa went over the rail.

Jamie appeared in the broken window, held beneath the arms by the last passenger. Saira looped the rope across his chest and tightened a knot.

Elias raised his good arm.

Jamie saw him.

For a moment his brother looked so young that Elias could remember carrying him on his shoulders, Jamie’s trainers knocking against his ribs.

Then Saira lowered him into the water.

The rope took his weight. His face stayed clear. Darren leaned down from the wall, reaching.

Jamie was on the line.

Not safe. Not dry.

But no longer Elias’s to hold.

Elias ducked under the yellow lintel.

The passage ceiling scraped his back. He moved by touch: left hand along brick, right hand finding the steel pipe that ran towards the release chamber. Two bends. A step down. No branching until the gate.

He had told Mara the route was clear on Thursday.

It had been clear enough for an inspection.

A loose cable caught his collar. He backed up, tore himself free and felt water pulse around him.

One stored breath emptied.

At the chamber, he surfaced into a pocket beneath the arched ceiling. His helmet knocked brick. He had lost the helmet somewhere, he realised; that was his skull.

The release wheel stood half underwater.

He braced a foot against its pedestal and turned.

Nothing.

“Come on.”

His voice sounded small in the enclosed air.

He shifted his grip, used both arms. Pain burned along the forearm that had held the seat. The wheel moved a quarter-turn, then another.

Behind the grille at the chamber’s far end, the gate rose.

Water hammered underneath it.

The level around Elias’s waist climbed to his chest in seconds. The wheel dragged against his hands. He turned again.

A crack sounded inside the housing.

The gate stopped.

Water screamed through an opening no higher than his boot.

He hauled on the wheel. It would move backwards, but not forwards.

He could close it. Preserve the passage. Go back to the ladder.

Above the wheel, a pressure dial shuddered near its red mark. The station interceptor was still full.

Elias let go and submerged.

The reserve opened around his mouth. He felt along the housing until he found the obstruction: the timber packing piece had shifted into the rack. The gate’s weight had driven a splintered end between the lifting teeth and the pawl bracket.

He pulled it.

It did not move.

He pulled harder, feet against the wall. A splinter entered his thumb. The wood stayed lodged.

The gate needed to rise another few inches before it would clear the emergency catch. Beyond that point, the counterweight would take it fully open.

The wheel could not lift it with the rack jammed.

But the bottom of the rack stood within a handspan of the pedestal.

Elias fitted his hand into the gap.

Wedge pushed.

The mechanism trembled.

His fingertips slid on grease. He reset them, palm against iron, and drove upwards.

The rack moved.

Not much. Enough for the timber to crack.

The pawl bracket bent outwards.

He stopped.

That bracket held the gate against the counterweight. Break it, and there would be no winding the gate shut again. No slow opening. No passage back.

His reserve yielded another breath.

He could feel the flow seeking the outfall behind him, tugging at his clothes.

He thought of the passengers stepping away from the window because he had said ten minutes.

This time he knew what would happen.

He pushed.

The bracket broke with a shock that travelled through his wrist.

The rack shot upwards.

Elias snatched his hand clear as the wheel spun. The gate slammed into its upper stops, and the chamber became a throat.

Water hit him broadside.

He caught the pedestal with both arms. His feet streamed behind him. Above the wheel, the pressure dial’s needle dropped out of the red.

It worked.

He saw that much before the current tore him loose.

Brick scraped his shoulder. Something struck his hip. He reached for the pipe, missed it, and was carried through the outfall mouth feet first.

The dark turned him over.

He spent one breath trying not to panic and another failing.

His fingers found seams that vanished before he could close his hand. The channel widened. For a moment he rolled without touching anything.

Then his back struck a sloping surface, and the water threw him upwards.

His last stored air thinned around his mouth.

He broke the surface coughing.

A ledge caught his forearms. He dragged himself onto it, unable to get his knees beneath him, and lay with his face against cold stone.

Breath Vault was empty.

He took air that smelled of wet copper.

The chamber around him was enormous.

Black columns rose out of the water, their sides scored with the same broad steps he had seen above the warehouses. A pale light travelled beneath their surfaces. Far overhead, water fell through narrow slots with the steady roar of heavy rain.

He was beneath the new obstruction.

Behind him, the outfall emptied through a hole twice his height. Even if he could reach it, the current would not let him enter.

Across the chamber stood an open doorway.

Something moved in it.

At first Elias thought the little lights belonged to the wall. Then the shape unfolded, lifting a long forelimb from the threshold.

Kernels studded its skin. Grey ones. Yellow ones. Clear ovals set deep between overlapping plates.

Its head turned towards his coughing.

Elias pushed himself backwards until his heel found the edge of the ledge.

Above him, Jamie still needed to reach dry ground.

The only way Elias could go was past the thing in the doorway.
